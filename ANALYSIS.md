# The English Modal System — a first-principles analysis

*The teacher-facing document behind **Fine Tuning**. Everything in the eight stages is derived
from what is set out here; if a module ever seems arbitrary, the reason for it is on this page.*

---

## 0. The one idea

**A modal verb does not describe the world. It describes the speaker's relationship to a
description of the world.**

Compare:

> It rains in April.
> It **may** rain in April.

The first sentence makes a claim about rainfall. The second makes no claim about rainfall at
all. It makes a claim about the *speaker* — about how far they are prepared to commit to the
proposition *it rains in April*. The rain is unchanged; the speaker has stepped back from it.

Every modal sentence therefore has two layers:

| layer | what it is | example |
|---|---|---|
| **the proposition** | the state of affairs being talked about | *the team win* |
| **the modal frame** | what the speaker is doing with that proposition — doubting it, deducing it, requiring it, permitting it, predicting it | *the team **must** win* |

This is not a metaphor. It is the reason for every grammatical peculiarity of the class. A
modal is not a verb in the ordinary sense; it is an **operator on a proposition**, standing
outside the proposition and looking in. Operators do not conjugate, do not stack, and do not
take a tense of their own — and English modals do none of those things.

Students who never separate the two layers experience modals as a list of words with
translations. Students who do separate them find that the entire system — negation, the past
forms, politeness, hedging, the semi-modals — falls out of a single idea. **That separation is
what Stage 1 exists to install and what every later stage depends on.**

## 1. Why the grammar is strange: modals are operators

English modals share a grammatical signature that no lexical verb has, traditionally summed up
as the **NICE** properties:

- **N**egation — they take *not* directly: *cannot*, *must not*. A lexical verb needs *do*.
- **I**nversion — they invert for questions: *Can she…?* A lexical verb needs *do*.
- **C**ode (ellipsis) — they survive on their own: *She can, and he can too.*
- **E**mphasis — they carry stress: *I **can** do it.*

Add four negative facts:

- no third-person *-s* (*he must*, never \**he musts*)
- no *to* after them (*must go*, never \**must to go*)
- no non-finite forms — no infinitive, no participle, no gerund
- **no stacking** (\**will can*, \**must can*)

And one positional fact: a modal is always **first** in the verb phrase, and the order of the
whole phrase is fixed:

> **MODAL → have (perfect) → be (progressive) → be (passive) → main verb**
>
> *might · have · been · being · examined*

Teaching this slot chain once explains every complex modal form a student will ever meet,
including the ones that look frightening. It is a single ordered chain, not a list of
"structures" to memorise. Stage 1 teaches the chain; Stage 8 stress-tests it.

### The defectiveness, and the repair

Because modals have no non-finite forms, English cannot say \**will can*, \**to must*,
\**has musted*. The language solved this by growing a parallel **periphrastic** system:

| modal | what it cannot do | the repair |
|---|---|---|
| can | \*will can, \*to can, \*have canned | **be able to** — *will be able to, to be able to, has been able to* |
| must | \*will must, \*to must, no past | **have to** — *will have to, to have to, had to* |
| may (permission) | \*will may | **be allowed to / be permitted to** |
| should | \*to should | **be supposed to / be expected to** |

**The semi-modal system is not an alternative vocabulary. It is a repair kit for a defective
class.** Once a student sees it that way, the choice between *must* and *have to* stops being a
coin-toss: in a slot where a modal is grammatically impossible, there is no choice at all.

Where both are possible, a genuine difference survives: *must* tends to carry the speaker's own
authority, *have to* an authority outside the speaker.

> *I must stop eating so much sugar.* (I have decided this about myself.)
> *I have to be at the clinic by eight.* (The appointment decided it.)

## 2. Two worlds, one set of words

Every core modal has at least two readings, and they belong to different domains.

> *He **must** be in the library.* — I deduce it. (**epistemic**: about knowledge)
> *He **must** be in the library by four.* — he is required to be. (**deontic**: about duty)

> *She **may** be waiting.* — perhaps she is. (epistemic)
> *You **may** wait here.* — you are permitted to. (deontic)

> *The machine **can** lift two tonnes.* — it has the power. (**dynamic**: about capacity)
> *You **can** go.* — you are permitted. (deontic)
> *Accidents **can** happen.* — they sometimes do. (general possibility)

This is not random polysemy. It is **one scale applied to different domains**:

|  | necessity (all the possibilities) | possibility (at least one) |
|---|---|---|
| **epistemic** — what my evidence allows | *must, will, can't* | *may, might, could* |
| **deontic** — what the rules allow | *must, have to, mustn't* | *may, can, be allowed to* |
| **dynamic** — what the subject or circumstances allow | *(will — willingness)* | *can, could, be able to* |

The logical core is that **necessity is universal quantification and possibility is existential
quantification over the possibilities left open**. *It must be raining* = in every situation
consistent with what I know, it is raining. *It may be raining* = in at least one. This is why
the two halves of the table behave so differently under negation, which is the next section and
the single most expensive area of the whole system.

The three-way split (epistemic / deontic / dynamic) is more accurate than the traditional
two-way one, and it earns its keep at C1, where resolving which reading is in play is a real
comprehension skill. Stage 2 teaches the epistemic scale, Stage 3 the deontic, Stage 4 the
dynamic, and Stage 8 teaches students to tell them apart under pressure.

## 3. The negation asymmetry

**The negation of a necessity is not the necessity of a negation.** This one sentence is worth
more marks than any other fact about modals.

When a modal is negated, *not* may attach either to the **modal** (external negation: *there is
no obligation*) or to the **proposition** (internal negation: *there is an obligation not to*).
English distributes the two unpredictably across the modals, and learners cannot guess it.

| form | what is negated | meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *You **mustn't** tell her.* | the proposition | it is required that you **not** tell |
| *You **don't have to** tell her.* | the modal | there is **no** requirement either way |
| *You **needn't** tell her.* | the modal | no requirement |
| *You **can't / may not** tell her.* | the proposition | it is forbidden |
| *You **shouldn't** tell her.* | the proposition | advice **not** to |

So *mustn't* and *don't have to* — which look like the affirmative-and-negative of a single
pair — are near-opposites. This is the **negation cliff**, and Stage 3 Level 2 is built entirely
around it.

On the epistemic side the asymmetry produces something stranger still: **suppletion**. The
negative of epistemic *must* is not *mustn't* but **can't**.

> *He must be at home.* → *He **can't** be at home.*
> (\**He mustn't be at home* is only a prohibition, never a deduction.)

And *may not / might not* are not the negatives of *may / might* in the same way — they are the
possibility of a negative:

> *She **may not** have seen it.* = it is possible she did not see it.
> *She **can't** have seen it.* = it is impossible that she saw it.

A student who treats those two as interchangeable has inverted the meaning. Stage 2 Level 2
exists for exactly this pair.

## 4. The past forms are not past tenses

*Could, might, would, should* are historically the past forms of *can, may, will, shall*.
Synchronically they are usually **not past at all**:

> *I **could** swim when I was six.* — past ability. (genuinely past)
> *You **could** be right.* — present, weak possibility.
> ***Could** you pass the salt?* — present, polite.

One form, three uses. The unifying idea is **distance**:

| distance in… | effect | example |
|---|---|---|
| **time** | past reference | *We could walk there when we lived closer.* |
| **likelihood** | tentativeness, unreality | *That could be the answer.* / *If they invested, they might recover.* |
| **social space** | politeness, reduced imposition | *Could you send it again?* |

**The past morphology marks remoteness, and remoteness is interpreted along whichever axis the
context makes available.** This is the same mechanism that produces the unreal past in
conditionals (*if I had more time*) and the subjunctive (*I'd rather you didn't*) — one
principle, three grammatical territories. It is also the engine of English politeness: the more
remote the form, the smaller the imposition.

> *Can you…?* → *Could you…?* → *Would you mind …ing?*

Stage 5 is this insight, whole.

### So how does English express past modality?

Because the modal cannot itself be tensed, **the tense is pushed into the proposition**:

> *He **must have left**.*

The modality is present — I am deducing *now*. The proposition is past — the leaving happened
*then*. The form **modal + have + past participle** is not "the past of must"; it is present
deduction about a past event. Once that is clear, the whole family follows:

| form | reading |
|---|---|
| *must have done* | confident deduction about the past |
| *can't / couldn't have done* | confident deduction that it did not happen |
| *may / might / could have done* | weak possibility about the past |
| *should / ought to have done* | it did not happen, and that is a fault — criticism, regret |
| *could have done* | the possibility that was available and not taken |
| *would have done* | the unreal consequent in past time |

And the famous pair, which distinguishes what happened from what was required:

> *You **needn't have** waited.* — you waited, **and it was unnecessary**.
> *You **didn't need to** wait.* — it was unnecessary (and normally, you didn't).

Stage 6 is this family.

## 5. The aspectual restriction on *could*

*Could* is the past of *can* for **general** ability, but it is blocked for a **single completed
achievement**:

> *She **could** play the violin at six.* ✓ (a standing capacity)
> \**She **could** win the final last Saturday.* ✗ → *She **was able to / managed to** win.*

The reason is aspectual: *could* is imperfective — it names a power, not an event. A single
achievement is perfective, and needs a form that asserts the event actually came off. English
supplies *was able to*, *managed to*, *succeeded in -ing*.

Two exceptions keep the rule honest:

- **perception and cognition verbs** are stative and so escape it: *From the balcony I could
  see the whole harbour*, *I could understand most of it*.
- **the negative** escapes it too, because a failure is not an achievement: *She couldn't win
  the final* is perfectly good.

This restriction is exactly the kind of thing that separates a B2 from a C1 candidate, and it is
invisible to a student who has only been given "could = past of can". Stage 4 Level 2.

## 6. *Will* is not a future tense

English has no future tense. *Will* is a modal — it sits in the modal slot, takes a bare
infinitive, and cannot stack. What it expresses is **prediction**, which is a stance, not a
time:

> *That'll be the postman.* — present deduction.
> *Oil will float on water.* — generic truth.
> *She will keep interrupting.* — characteristic behaviour, usually with irritation.
> *The results will be published in June.* — prediction about the future.

The future reading is the most common but not the defining one. This explains a rule students
usually meet as an arbitrary prohibition: \**if it will rain* is bad because the *if* already
supplies the modality — the clause is about a possibility, and it does not need a second
operator to say so. (When *will* survives in an *if*-clause it is doing something else:
*If you'll wait here* = willingness.)

Stage 8 Level 3.

## 7. Modality as calibration: the academic core

This is where the system pays for itself in IELTS, and it is the reason Stage 7 exists.

Academic English is saturated with modality — not from timidity, but because **a claim must be
pitched at the strength the evidence will bear.** Modality is how a writer says *how much* they
are asserting.

> *This proves that smaller classes raise attainment.*
> *This **suggests** that smaller classes **may** raise attainment.*

The first sentence will be wrong more often than it is right, and an examiner reads it as a
writer who cannot tell a correlation from a cause. The second is the same finding, correctly
calibrated. Band 7 and above is largely the ability to **match the force of the claim to the
strength of the evidence**, and modals are the primary instrument for doing it.

Two failures, both penalised:

- **Overclaiming** — *proves, will always, everyone, must be the case* on thin evidence.
- **The hedge pile-up** — *it may possibly perhaps be somewhat arguable that…*, which reads as
  a writer with nothing to say.

The toolkit is layered, and the layers combine:

| layer | examples |
|---|---|
| modal | *may, might, could, would, should, tends to* |
| modal + adverb | *may well, might conceivably, would almost certainly, could arguably* |
| lexical verb | *suggest, indicate, appear, seem, tend to* |
| impersonal frame | *it may be argued that, it would appear that, it is widely held that* |
| approximator | *in most cases, broadly, to some extent, on the whole* |
| booster (the opposite move) | *clearly, undoubtedly, must, certainly* — **earned**, never decorative |

The skilled move in a Task 2 essay is the **concessive modal**: granting the other side at a
calibrated strength before qualifying it.

> *While automation **may well** displace routine roles in the short term, it **is unlikely to**
> eliminate the need for human judgement in the professions.*

That sentence concedes, scales, and counters in one move, and it is built entirely out of
modality. Stage 7 teaches students to construct it deliberately.

## 8. What Thai-speaking learners bring to this

Thai marks modality with preverbal and sentence-final particles and with serial verbs
(*ต้อง* = must, *อาจ* = may, *สามารถ…ได้* = be able to, *ควร* = should). There is no defective
auxiliary class, so nothing in the L1 predicts the English signature. The predictable
consequences, in rough order of frequency:

1. \**He can to go* / \**must to* — *to* inserted, because the Thai pattern is verb + verb.
2. \**He will can come* — stacking, because Thai modality is freely serial.
3. \**He musts* — third-person *-s* generalised from lexical verbs.
4. *mustn't* used for *don't have to* — the negation cliff, which no L1 cue warns about.
5. *can* used for permission, ability, possibility and request with no register grading —
   the politeness dial is never turned.
6. Past modality expressed by adding a past adverbial rather than by *have* + participle:
   \**He must go yesterday*.
7. Flat assertion in writing where English requires hedging, because Thai academic register
   achieves the same effect lexically rather than grammatically. This reads as overclaiming.

Each of these is a designed target somewhere in the ladder rather than an incidental correction:
1–3 in Stage 1, 4 in Stage 3, 5 in Stage 5, 6 in Stage 6, 7 in Stage 7.

## 9. The ladder that follows from all this

| stage | title | CEFR | the idea it installs |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The Modal Frame | B1–B1+ | Proposition vs frame; the signature; the slot chain; why the semi-modals exist |
| 2 | The Ladder of Certainty | B1+–B2 | The epistemic scale, and *must* → *can't* |
| 3 | Obligation and Permission | B2 | The deontic scale, and the negation cliff |
| 4 | Ability and Willingness | B2–B2+ | Dynamic modality, and the single-occasion rule |
| 5 | Distance | B2+–C1 | Past form = remoteness in time, likelihood or social space |
| 6 | Modality in Past Time | B2+–C1 | The tense belongs to the proposition: modal + *have* + participle |
| 7 | Hedging and Stance | C1 | Matching claim strength to evidence — the IELTS core |
| 8 | The Whole System | C1–C1+ | Resolving ambiguity; complex chains; the periphery; stance across a text |

Stages 1–4 build the three domains one at a time. Stage 5 supplies the mechanism (distance)
that Stages 6–8 all draw on. Stage 7 is where the grammar becomes a writing skill. Stage 8 is
where the student is asked to hold the whole thing at once.

The triage test samples all eight so that a student who already owns Stages 1–4 is sent
straight to 5–8 instead of grinding through material they do not need.
